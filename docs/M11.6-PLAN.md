# M11.6 plan: services that outlive their process

Status (2026-10-02): a plan, nothing built. Written against main e61faab.
**Waiting for the owner's answers to the questions below before any
code.** It is the first of wave 1's three plans to need the owner
([PLAN-M11-M12.5.md](PLAN-M11-M12.5.md#wave-1-m11-m115-and-m116-together)):
M11 and M11.5 change mechanisms, this one changes what a service *is*.

Goal ([roadmap](ROADMAP.md#later)): **a service's queues and state live
outside its process, so a restarted service carries on where the dead one
stopped and its clients never see the crash; a warm spare makes the
restart fast. First fat and the mixer, then the drivers.**

**Done when** (the roadmap's row, unchanged):
- killing fat or the mixer during use is not seen by their clients: no
  error, no lost data, no gap in the sound;
- kill-to-first-answer is measured and in [BENCH.md](BENCH.md);
- the demonstration: a file copy on the PC that ends with the right
  checksum while fat is killed again and again, its throughput printed
  next to the kill rate;
- and, as every milestone: All tests and `soak 10` on the PC.

## What a restart looks like today

**fat.** devmgr notices the death, waits 100 ms (`SUP_BACKOFF_FIRST`),
opens a new `block` channel and starts a new fat on a **new** `fs`
channel (`schedule()` in `user/services/devmgr/supervise.c` closes the
old client end and makes a new pair). The old channel, every open file's
channel and every view die with the process: clients get
`ERR_PEER_CLOSED`, init sends every namespace the new mount, programs
reopen. A write that fat answered but had not synced is lost: the
directory entry still has the size of the last sync, and the clusters
written since are allocated to nothing. A sixth death in a minute and
devmgr gives up.

**The mixer.** init already keeps the server ends of `/svc/audio` and
`/svc/audioctl` (`user/services/init/services.c`), so new opens wait for
the restart. But each opener's channel, each stream (its channel, ring
and event) and the hda driver's stream channel die with it. The driver
stops and mutes its stream at once (its stream channel closed), so the
sound stops; init waits at least 100 ms; programs see `ERR_PEER_CLOSED`
and open new streams (the music player reopens, `play` fails). The
eleventh end in a minute and init gives up.

## Questions for the owner

Each question stands on its own, says what it decides and why it
matters, and ends with a recommendation. The plan assumes the
recommendation until you say otherwise. Q1 to Q3 shape everything; Q4 to
Q8 are smaller.

### Q1. What exactly may a client notice when a service is killed?

"Clients never see the crash" can mean two different things, and the
choice decides how much the services must remember.

- **(A) Exactly once, invisible.** A client sees only a slower answer.
  Every call fat or the mixer answered stays done (an answered write is
  never lost), and the call that was in progress when the service died
  is finished exactly once by the successor, which re-runs it from saved
  state. No error, no new status, no change in libos.
- **(B) At least once.** Calls in progress may run twice. Only methods
  marked `idempotent` in the IDL (a read, a write at an explicit offset)
  are re-run; the others fail with a new error ("the service restarted,
  ask again") that libos retries where it can. Simpler services, but a
  client can see an error (an `unlink` whose first run deleted the file
  gets `ERR_NOT_FOUND` the second time) and an answered-but-unsynced
  write can still be lost unless fat syncs on every write.

One exception either way, against a bad request: if the service
**crashes** (not a deliberate kill) twice while the same request is in
progress, that request is answered with an error (`ERR_IO` for fat,
`ERR_INTERNAL` for the mixer) and dropped, and the service carries on.
Otherwise one crafted request would crash every successor in turn and
take the service away from everyone.

*Recommendation: (A) for fat and the mixer*, with the bad-request
exception. The done-when ("no error", "right checksum") needs it, and
the design below gets it without libos knowing anything happened. (B)
stays as the rule for services that keep no state (the drivers, later).

### Q2. Where do the handles a service made live while it is dead?

fat makes a channel and a buffer VMO for every open file and a channel
for every view; the mixer makes a channel, a ring VMO and an event for
every stream and a channel for every opener, and holds the hda driver's
stream channel. When a process dies the kernel closes every handle it
held, and a channel end with no handle left closes, so the client sees
`ERR_PEER_CLOSED`. To survive, those objects need a handle somewhere
else while the service is dead.

- **(A) The supervisor keeps a duplicate.** Each time the service makes
  such a handle it sends a duplicate to its supervisor (devmgr for fat,
  init for the mixer) on a "keep" channel, and says when it closes it;
  a successor is sent them all back. No kernel change: channels, VMOs
  and events can already be duplicated and sent, and init already does
  exactly this for the mixer's two shared channels. Costs one message
  per open and per close (about 1 to 2 us each).
- **(B) The kernel hands the dead process's whole handle table to its
  successor** ("adopt"). The successor finds every handle at its old
  value and no messages are needed. But it is new kernel machinery in
  the most delicate path there is (process teardown, which today
  promises that `SIG_TERMINATED` means "everything given back"), the
  job charges would have to move between jobs, and for drivers it would
  carry over exactly what must not survive (the `dma_cap` and the
  interrupt: a driver's restart is a bind from scratch).

*Recommendation: (A).* By Liedtke's test it belongs in a process: one
can do it with the handles it has. Its cost is small and only on open
and close, and it can start now. (B) can be looked at again by M12's
kernel track if the keep messages ever show up in a profile.

### Q3. What does fat keep, so that an answered write survives fat?

What fat has answered but not yet put on the stick lives in fat's
memory: FatFs's sector window (a changed FAT sector), each open file's
sector buffer, size and cluster chain (the directory entry is only
updated at sync), and the held writes of an `FS_GATHER` file
(`user/services/fat/hold.c`). Three ways to keep it:

- **(A) fat's working state lives in a VMO, and every request's disk
  writes are held until the request is complete.** FatFs's volume
  struct, every open file's struct, fat's tables and the hold all live
  in a "state VMO" that the supervisor keeps. Every write a request
  makes goes into the hold (in that VMO) instead of to the stick; when
  the request is done, fat marks it committed (one word) and only then
  sends the held sectors out, then answers. A successor finds either an
  uncommitted request (nothing of it reached the stick: it puts the
  memory back as it was before the request and runs it again) or a
  committed one (it sends the held sectors out again, which is harmless,
  and answers). Exact: no lost write, no lost cluster, no damage, and no
  disk reads at a restart. The cost while nothing dies is one extra copy
  of written data into the hold and a few KiB copied per request (about
  a microsecond). It relies on FatFs's internals (which structs hold
  its state; FatFs is pinned at R0.16 and a test checks the layout) and
  is the most work.
- **(B) Every write reaches usb-storage before it is answered.** fat
  syncs the file after every write (FatFs's `f_sync`, without the SCSI
  cache flush: the stick's own cache outlives fat) and keeps only its
  table of open files; a successor mounts again and reopens them by
  path. Simple, but each write call then costs two more small writes on
  the stick (the FAT sector and the directory entry; a cheap stick takes
  milliseconds for each, `user/services/fat/hold.c` has the numbers), so
  copies get slower even when nothing dies, a successor must read the
  disk to reopen (milliseconds), a write in progress can leave a cluster
  allocated to nothing, and `FS_GATHER` (update -w) loses what it held.
- **(C) Keep today's promise** (unsynced writes may be lost). Fails the
  done-when: the copy's checksum would be wrong.

*Recommendation: (A)*, built in steps that each work on their own
(stages F1 to F3), with (B) as the fallback if the kill-storm checker
ever finds damage. One corner stays: a request whose writes don't fit in
the hold (about 1 MiB of sectors: only an `unlink` or `truncate` of a
file over about 2 GiB on /data, whose freed chain spans more FAT
sectors than that) goes out in steps, and a death between steps can
leave the rest of that file's clusters allocated to nothing: lost space,
said in the log, never a damaged file.

### Q4. A warm spare: always running, and for which services?

A warm spare is a second copy of the service, already loaded and
started, blocked before it has looked at anything (no disk read, no
device), holding nothing but one channel from its supervisor. When the
service dies, the supervisor hands the spare everything in one message
and it carries on at once; then a new spare starts in the background.
Without one, a restart includes starting a process (loading the program,
its first page faults), which is milliseconds; with one, it is
microseconds plus whatever the request in progress needs from the
device. The mixer needs it most: the sound already written ahead lasts
128 to 171 ms (four periods of 42.7 ms), and the successor must write
the next period before that runs out.

A spare is not tied to a mount until it is promoted, so **one spare fat
serves every mount** (/data, /esp, every /usbN), and one spare mixer
serves the mixer. Cost: two processes that do nothing (each an estimated
150 to 300 KiB of memory plus a blocked thread's 68 KiB of kernel stack
and FPU area; stages S3a and S3b measure it), always.

*Recommendation: yes, always on: one spare fat and one spare mixer*,
with a boot word `nospare` to turn them off, so BENCH.md can show both
(as every other optimisation that has a switch). The drivers get spares
when their turn comes (Q8).

### Q5. How should supervision count deliberate kills?

The demonstration kills fat many times a second. Today devmgr gives up
on a service after its sixth death in 60 s and init after the eleventh,
and every restart waits at least 100 ms. Those rules are there to stop a
service that crashes at every start from running forever.

*Recommendation:* a **deliberate kill** (the shell's `kill`, the storm
below, `DEVMGR_KILL`) neither counts toward giving up nor waits: the
spare is promoted at once. A **crash** (the kernel killed it, or it
exited with an error) still counts as today (5 in 60 s at devmgr, 10 at
init); the first crash in a window is restarted at once by the spare,
later ones back off as today (100 ms doubling to 5 s). With Q1's
bad-request rule a crash loop on one request ends after two crashes
without anyone giving up.

### Q6. A requirement on M11.5's fast path

M11.5 is rebuilding the call path (one reply-and-wait call, a direct
hand-off to a waiting server, one copy). Some fast-IPC designs (seL4's
reply objects) make the right to answer a call belong to the server
**thread** that received it: when that thread dies, the call can only
fail. This plan needs the opposite: a call stays answerable **on its
channel end, by its txid**, as today, so that a successor holding the
same (kept) channel end can answer a call its predecessor took. Two
small extras would close the last gaps (below, "The request in
progress"): the receive half may copy into a buffer the server chooses
(its state VMO), and the call can write "the reply went out" into the
server's memory before it waits for the next request.

*Recommendation:* ask the M11.5 plan to keep replies addressed by
(channel end, txid) and to offer the two extras. Speed doesn't depend on
it: a direct hand-off can still switch straight to the waiting caller.

### Q7. The demonstration: what is copied, what is killed, how fast

*Recommendation:* one shell command, `storm`, that copies a file while a
thread of its own kills services at a fixed rate, then prints the
throughput, the kills, kill-to-first-answer (median, p99, worst) and the
SHA-256 of both files. On the PC: a 256 MiB file from /usb0 (the
SanDisk) to /data, killing **fat-data and fat-usb0 alternately** (both
the reading and the writing side), at 0, 1, 10 and 100 kills a second;
the four lines go into BENCH.md. Afterwards the stick goes to the Mac
for `fsck_msdos -n` on /data (no damage, no lost clusters). For the
mixer: `music start`, then `storm mixer 2 60` (two kills a second for a
minute) while you listen, plus the mixer's own counters (below) as the
objective check.

### Q8. When do the drivers get their turn?

The roadmap says "first fat and the mixer, then the drivers". Drivers
are harder: their state is partly in the device (the hda codec's path,
usb-storage's SCSI state, the NIC's rings), and their restart is a bind
from scratch on purpose (a new `dma_cap`, bus mastering off until the
device is quiet: the safe rebind). With M11's IOMMU that rule gets
stronger, not weaker.

*Recommendation:* M11.6's done-when covers fat and the mixer only. This
plan adds the one driver change the services need (the usb-storage
fence, below) and, as its last stage, a design note for the drivers
(usb-storage first: it keeps no state and its block calls are
idempotent, so its clients can be kept waiting while the USB reset runs;
then hda, whose stream could be re-created at the same position).
Building them is a milestone of its own after M12, so M12 reviews
fat's and the mixer's interfaces before drivers copy them.

## The design in one page

A service that outlives its process keeps three kinds of state apart:

1. **Kernel objects clients hold the other end of** (channels, ring
   VMOs, events): **kept alive by the supervisor**, which holds a
   duplicate of each (Q2). Clients' handles never change, so clients
   never notice.
2. **What the service knows** (open files, positions, gains, what is
   held for the stick): **in a state VMO** the supervisor makes, keeps
   and hands to each successor, committed at the end of every request
   (fat) or period (the mixer). The supervisor never maps it; clients
   never see it.
3. **Everything else** (the port and its bindings, fat's block cache,
   the mixer's scratch buffers, the mapping of the block buffer):
   **rebuilt** by the successor, in microseconds.

The **request in progress** is read straight into the state VMO, so it
survives too: a successor re-runs it from the last committed state, or,
if it had committed, finishes it, and answers on the kept channel with
the caller's txid. The caller's `channel_call` simply returns later.

A **warm spare** waits, blocked, holding nothing; the supervisor
promotes it with one message. **usb-storage** never starts a request
from a client that has died, so nothing a dead fat queued can land
after its successor's writes.

```
            client                     supervisor (devmgr / init)
              |  channel_call              | keeps: dup of every server end,
              v                            |        the state VMO, the spare
   [ kept channel end ] <--- dup ---------+
              |                            |  on death: promote spare,
              v                            |  hand over kept handles + state
   service process (fat / mixer) ----------+
     - reads each request into its state VMO slot
     - runs it, holding every disk write
     - commits (one word), sends what it held, answers
```

## Where each service's state lives

### fat

| State | Where it lives | After a restart |
|---|---|---|
| The `fs` channel (the mount) | devmgr keeps the server end across restarts (as init does for the mixer's) | the same channel; namespaces never change |
| Each view (`fs.view`) | its channel kept (keep channel); its flags in the state VMO | the same channel, the same flags |
| Each open file: its channel and 64 KiB buffer VMO | kept (keep channel) | the same handles; the client's mapping never moved |
| Each open file: FatFs's `FIL` (size, position, chain, dirty sector buffer, where its directory entry is), fat's slot (flags, sharing count, path) | the state VMO | as committed by the last request |
| The volume: FatFs's `FATFS` (geometry, the sector window and its dirty flag, the free-cluster hint) | the state VMO, at a fixed address (FatFs's structs point into each other) | as committed; FatFs is told the volume is mounted without reading the disk (below) |
| Held writes (`FS_GATHER` files, and from now on every request's writes until it commits) | the state VMO (the hold: about 1.1 MiB, committed only as used) | committed ones kept and sent; uncommitted ones dropped |
| The dirty flag (FAT[1]'s clean bit: what fat believes the stick says) | the state VMO (fat's `vol` fields that aren't handles) | as committed: no "volume found dirty" line, no remount |
| The request in progress, its data, its reply | a slot in the state VMO (two, alternating) | re-run or finished, then answered |
| The block cache (`user/services/fat/cache.c`, 16 lines of 64 KiB) | fat's own memory | **rebuilt cold.** It is write-through and could hold sectors of the uncommitted request, so it is dropped; the next reads refill it |
| Directory cursors (`user/services/fat/dirs.c`) | fat's own memory | rebuilt: a listing in progress goes on from a fresh walk (`fs.readdir` takes an index) |
| The `block` channel and its mapped buffer | fat's own | a new channel from devmgr (opened in advance for the spare), mapped again |
| `fsctl` | devmgr makes a new one per start, as today | new |

**Held writes become the write-ahead buffer.** Today the hold exists to
make `update -w` fast. Here every request runs with the hold on: FatFs's
disk writes go into it, reads see them laid over the disk
(`hold_overlay`, as today), and nothing of the request reaches the stick
before it commits. That is what makes a re-run exact: the disk is
exactly as the previous request left it, and memory is put back to the
same point, so FatFs makes the same decisions again. After the commit
the held sectors go out in the order they were held (data, then FAT,
then directory: today's order) in 64 KiB writes, then the answer.
`FS_GATHER` keeps its meaning: its writes stay held across requests
until its sync or close (or the hold fills), and now survive a restart
too. A request never sends held sectors out half way: if the hold lacks
room for a whole request at its start, what is committed goes out first.
The one exception is Q3's: an `unlink` or `truncate` of a file whose
freed chain needs more FAT sectors than the hold has.

**The write in progress, exactly once.** A successor finds one of four
cases in the slot: nothing (idle); taken but not committed (memory is
put back from the undo copy, held sectors past the committed mark are
dropped, the request runs again from its saved bytes); committed (the
held sectors are sent again, which writes the same bytes, then the saved
reply); sent (only the reply). The request's data (a `file.write`'s
bytes) is copied into the slot before it runs (it already goes through a
bounce buffer: the bounce *is* the slot), so a re-run writes the same
bytes even if the client has changed its buffer since.

**FatFs without re-mounting.** FatFs keeps one pointer to the volume
outside our structs (its static `FatFs[]`). A successor sets it with
`f_mount(&state->fs, "", 0)` (which only registers the struct) and then
copies the committed `FATFS` back over it, so FatFs believes it is
mounted, with the same window and mount id the open files carry. FatFs's
own lock table (`FF_FS_LOCK`, `user/services/fat/ffport/ffconf.h`) is
the one other piece of state it keeps on its own; it goes (set to 0), and
fat does its job itself: a second writer, an `unlink` or `rename` of an
open file refused by fat's table, matched by directory entry (sector and
offset) rather than by name, so an 8.3 alias can't open a file twice. A
utest checks every field this relies on against the vendored FatFs.

**The undo copy.** Before a request runs, fat copies what it can change:
`FATFS`, the one `FIL` of a `file` request (FatFs touches no other), or
fat's tables for an `fs` request or a close, and the hold's counters
(held sectors that an uncommitted request overwrites in place are saved
too, or appended instead of overwritten: the track's choice, with a
test). About 1 to 4 KiB per request. The commit is a single aligned
store (a released atomic) that invalidates the undo copy; a process is
killed between instructions, never inside one, so the word is either old
or new.

### The mixer

| State | Where it lives | After a restart |
|---|---|---|
| `/svc/audio`, `/svc/audioctl` | init keeps the server ends (as today) | the same |
| Each opener's channel (`svc.connect`) | kept (keep channel); its slot in the state VMO | the same |
| Each stream's channel, ring VMO and event | kept; the stream's numbers in the state VMO | the same handles, the same ring; the client never stops writing |
| Each stream's numbers: id, name, owner, volume, `read` (frames taken), the history for `played`, drain in progress (its txid), counters | the state VMO | as committed |
| The hda driver's channel, its stream channel and ring | kept (the stream channel above all: if it closed the driver would stop and mute) | the same stream, still running; the ring mapped again |
| The output's numbers: frames written, play position, period, bits, the wait_period's txid, the limiter's state, the dither seed | the state VMO | as committed; a wait_period answer that arrives meanwhile waits in the kept stream channel and is taken by the successor (its txid is the mixer's own range) |
| Master volume, next id, next txid | the state VMO | as committed |
| An `audioctl.device` in progress (device.c's thread: it calls devmgr and the driver) | a slot of its own in the state VMO | run again (it makes a new query channel; its reply carries a handle, so the rule for such replies below applies) |
| The mixing buffers, the port | the mixer's own | rebuilt |

**Committing a period.** The order is: mix the period, write it into
the driver's ring, commit, then publish each stream's new `read` into
its ring header. A death before the commit leaves `read` unpublished, so
the clients' frames are still in their rings; the successor mixes the
same period again from the committed numbers (the same frames, gains,
limiter state and dither seed: the same samples, bit for bit) and writes
it over the same place in the driver's ring, which is still ahead of the
play position. A death after the commit and before the publish: the
successor publishes. A control call (volume, start, stop, open, close)
commits before it answers. So nothing ever plays twice or goes missing,
and the only thing that can cost a gap is time: the successor must write
the next period before the 128 ms already written ahead are played.

**What the mixer never trusts.** The client writes the ring header too.
The successor takes each stream's `read` from its state, never from the
header (today's mixer already keeps its own copy), and reads the client's
`write` as today, clamped.

## How a successor is handed its state

### The keep channel

A small protocol in libos, both sides (new files user/include/keep.h and
user/lib/keep.c), written by hand like the namespace's `NS_SET` because
it carries handles in one-way messages, which genidl refuses today
(M12's protocol track can move it into IDL):

- service to supervisor: `KEEP_PUT(slot, n handles)` (a duplicate of
  each, same rights), `KEEP_DROP(slot)`;
- supervisor to successor: `KEEP_RESTORE(slot, handles)` messages, up to
  64 handles each, then `KEEP_DONE`.

Order makes it safe without a reply: fat sends `KEEP_PUT` before it
records the slot in its state and before it answers, and `KEEP_DROP`
after it has forgotten the slot. A successor closes any restored slot
its state doesn't know (a death between the two), and drops from its
state any slot the keeper didn't return. The keeper (devmgr's and init's
side, the same library code) takes channels, VMOs and events only,
never widens rights, refuses more than 256 slots per service, and holds
them in its own job. It hands them only to the next instance of the same
binding, closes them all when the service is given up on, retired (its
disk gone, a remount) or stopped in order (`fsctl.stop`): then clients
see `ERR_PEER_CLOSED`, today's behaviour.

The startup message holds at most 16 handles
(`kernel/include/jam/startup.h`), so kept handles come on the keep
channel, not in it.

### The state VMO

New files user/include/svcstate.h and user/lib/svcstate.c. The
supervisor makes the VMO (sized by the service: about 1.3 MiB for fat,
mostly the hold, committed only as used; tens of KiB for the mixer),
hands it over with read, write and map but not resize, transfer or
duplicate, and keeps its own handle without mapping it. The service maps
it at a fixed address (`VMAR_FIXED`, an address svcstate.h reserves)
before anything else, so pointers inside it stay valid. Its header:
magic, layout version (from the build), the binding it belongs to (for
fat: the disk's id and the partition), how many times it was adopted,
the two request slots and the commit word. A successor checks all of it
and every count and index inside; any mismatch, and it **starts fresh**
(mounts from the disk, closes what was kept, today's behaviour) with a
line in the log. The supervisor drops the VMO when the binding retires,
so a state can never reach a fat on another disk.

### The request in progress

Each service reads requests itself, into a slot of its state VMO, and
dispatches them with the generated `<proto>_dispatch_on` (which already
takes a request buffer and fills a reply buffer: `drivers/include/idl/file.h`
has `file_dispatch_on`), instead of `<proto>_serve_one`'s stack buffer.
Before the read it records in the slot which channel it reads from and
zeroes the slot's length; the kernel copies the message and its length
into the slot within one system call, and a killed thread always finishes the call it is in
before it dies (the cancel is acted on just before returning to user
mode, `kernel/arch/x86_64/uentry.c`), so a request is either still
queued or wholly in the slot. Stage S2 adds a kernel test that kills a
thread inside `channel_read` under load and checks exactly that. The
slot's pages are committed in advance, so the copy never faults.

Answering: commit, send what was held, write the reply. Two slots
alternate, so a request found in the other slot proves the previous
reply went out. One window stays: killed after the reply was written
and before the next request was read. The successor then answers again,
and the client gets a second reply with a txid it no longer waits for.
That is harmless: a blocking call matches only its own txid (the stray
reply stays queued and is freed with the channel), and a loop that sends
without waiting checks its txids (devmgr's `ask_stat` does). M11.5's
reply-and-wait closes the window if it writes "replied" into the
server's memory before it waits (Q6).

**A reply that carries handles** (`fs.open`, `fs.view`, `audio.open_output`,
`svc.connect`) can't be sent twice: the client end it carried went with
it. The successor tells by the kept server end: if its peer is alive,
the reply arrived (the client end exists somewhere); if the peer is
closed, it never did (the end died with the process), so the slot is
closed and the request runs again.

### Why clients don't reconnect themselves

The other way is for libos to notice the restart and redo things: reopen
each file by its path, re-send the call in progress. It was considered
and rejected for fat and the mixer:

- libos would have to keep a copy of every byte written since the last
  sync, or make fat sync every write (Q3's (B));
- a path is not an identity: the file may have been renamed, or opened
  through its 8.3 alias;
- views and per-opener channels are made by the program's starter;
  re-deriving them means another round of namespace updates;
- a new mixer stream starts at frame 0 with an empty ring: a gap.

libos's reconnect rules stay for services that keep no state, and as
the fallback when a successor starts fresh.

### What genidl generates

Not required for the first stages (they use `<proto>_dispatch_on` by
hand). Stage G adds, for every protocol, `<PROTO>_REQ_MAX` (the largest
request, for sizing slots), `<proto>_take(ch, slot)` (read one request
into a slot, with the key-and-zero step above) and `<proto>_run(slot,
ops, ctx, reply)` (dispatch a slot), so a service's loop and its
successor's re-run are the same generated code. It also adds a method
keyword, `idempotent` (running it again on the state it changed gives
the same result and the same answer: `block.read`, `block.write`,
`file.read`, `file.write` without `FS_APPEND`, every `stat`), recorded in
the generated header (`<proto>_idempotent(ordinal)`). fat and the mixer
don't need it (they undo, so any method re-runs exactly); the drivers
will (Q8): a stateless driver re-runs an idempotent request and answers
any other with an error. `tools/genidl.py` checks that every protocol's
header comment states its restart rule.

## The warm spare

- **Standby in libos** (`user/lib/start.c`): a program started with a
  startup role SR_STANDBY (new) blocks in libos's startup, before
  `main`, on that channel. The promotion message carries the handles by
  role, as a startup message does (up to 64), plus the kill's timestamp;
  libos adds them to its startup table, so `startup_handle(FAT_SR_BLOCK)`
  works afterwards and the service's code doesn't know it was a spare.
  Before promotion it holds nothing but that channel, has opened nothing
  and read nothing (the only safe point to wait at: a spare that had
  mounted would hold a copy of the disk that later writes make stale).
- **devmgr** keeps one spare fat, not bound to any mount. When a fat
  dies it opens a new `block` channel for that partition (it opens it in
  advance, when it starts the spare, for /data), hands the spare the
  role handles (`block`, the kept `fs` server end, a new `fsctl`, the
  state VMO, a new keep channel), then the kept handles, then starts the
  next spare. When fat starts the first time (or fresh) it gets an empty
  state VMO and does today's mount.
- **init** does the same for the mixer.
- `nospare` at boot: restarts start a process, as today, but still hand
  over the state (stages S3a and S3b measure both).

## The usb-storage fence

A channel's queued messages can still be read after its peer has
closed. So a `block.write` that a dying fat had queued but usb-storage
had not yet started could run **after** the successor's writes to the
same sector (with a spare that is microseconds later) and put an older
FAT sector back: real damage. fat makes one block call at a time, so at
most one such request exists. The fence: `drivers/usb-storage/block.c`
checks the channel's peer before it starts each request and drops it
unanswered if the client is gone. A request already started finishes
first, and usb-storage's single loop serves the successor's new channel
only after it, so the order holds. This is worth doing even before the
rest: today's 100 ms backoff only hides the race.

## Supervision

As Q5. In addition: a service that dies while its supervisor hands it
over (a spare dying mid-promotion) is a crash like any other; a
successor that finds its state unusable starts fresh and says so; a
supervisor's own death still takes its services down (devmgr's job holds
every fat), and their clients see `ERR_PEER_CLOSED` as today. Surviving
devmgr or init is not part of M11.6.

## Security

What must hold: **a restarted service gains nothing it didn't have, and
no client can forge the state a service is handed back.**

- **The successor's authority** is that of a fresh start: the same
  startup handles, minted again by the supervisor (never taken from the
  dead process), plus its own state VMO and the duplicates the dead
  instance itself handed to the keeper. Kept handles are only what that
  service made from its own authority, at the rights it had; the keeper
  refuses any other type (no `dma_cap`, interrupt, resource, process or
  job) and never widens rights.
- **The spare** holds only its standby channel, whose other end is the
  supervisor's alone, until promoted: it can't be used to reach anything.
- **The state VMO** is never handed to a client and never mapped by the
  supervisor; the service gets no transfer or duplicate right on it, so it
  can't pass it on, and no resize right. Nothing authority-bearing lives
  in memory a client can write: view and open flags come from the state,
  never from a request; the mixer's `read` from the state, never from the
  ring header; a re-run request's data from the slot copy, never re-read
  from the client's buffer; and a re-run goes through every check its
  first run did.
- **A state written by a crashing service** is checked before use;
  inconsistent means fresh start. A fat corrupted by an exploit already
  held the volume's `block` channel, so a bad state can give it nothing
  more; the state is bound to its disk and partition and dropped when they
  go.
- **A bad request** can't crash a service forever (Q1's rule), and crash
  loops still end supervision (Q5).
- **The fence** keeps a dead client's writes from landing late.
- **Limits:** 256 kept handles per service, charged to the supervisor's
  job; the state VMO's pages to the supervisor's job (it made it). Kept
  objects the dead instance created (a file's buffer VMO) stay charged to
  its dead job until they are closed: bounded by the same cap. Whether
  charges should move to the successor's job is a question for M12's
  kernel track.

## Kill-to-first-answer

**Definition:** as a client feels it. `storm`'s kill thread reads the
clock, asks for the kill (`initctl.kill`, which returns once the process
is dead), at once makes one cheap call on the killed service (`fs.statfs`
on the mount, or `stream_position` on a stream of its own) and reads the
clock when that call is answered: the difference is one sample. It
includes the kill path itself, the promotion and the request the
successor finishes first. The successor also logs where the time went,
from the supervisor's `process_kill` timestamp carried in its promotion
message: death seen, promoted, state checked and handles bound, the
request in progress finished, first answer.

**Targets** (estimates to be replaced by measurements):

| Path | Target, PC | Today |
|---|---|---|
| fat, warm spare, nothing in progress | median under 0.5 ms, p99 under 2 ms | over 100 ms, and clients see errors |
| fat, warm spare, a write in progress | the above plus that write's device time | as above |
| fat, `nospare` | the above plus a process start (measured) | |
| mixer, warm spare: kill to the next period written | median under 1 ms, worst under 20 ms (the budget is about 122 ms: 128 ms ahead minus the 5.3 ms guard) | the sound stops; over 100 ms |

The parts, estimated: the kill and the dead process's teardown (tens to
hundreds of microseconds: its handle table and address space), devmgr
or init seeing it (about 2 us), the promotion message and the kept
handles (64 per message), the successor's checks and port bindings
(tens of microseconds), the request in progress. BENCH.md gets a section
"M11.6" with these lines, the copy throughput per kill rate (Q7), and the
cost when nothing dies: a cached 4 KiB read and a 64 KiB write through
fat with and without the state (fat keeps a test switch that skips the
undo copy), next to M11.5's per-operation lines.

## The demonstration and its tests

**`storm`** (a shell command, user/services/shell/cmd/storm.c, using
init's control channel, which the shell already holds): `storm
<service...> <kills per second> <seconds>`, or with `-c <from> <to>`: copy
the file while killing, stop when the copy ends. The kills run on a
thread of its own that serves nothing (the loop rule allows it). It
prints the throughput, the number of kills, kill-to-first-answer
(median, p99, worst) and the SHA-256 of both files (`sha256sum`'s code).

**QEMU, fat** (tools/fat-storm-test.sh, at `QEMU_SMP=2`): the host writes
a 32 MiB file of random bytes into the data disk image before the boot
and remembers its SHA-256; the guest runs `storm -c` at 0, 10 and 50
kills a second (QEMU is slower than the PC), within one data partition
and from a second stick image; then the host checks the copies' SHA-256
and runs tools/fatcheck.py (new) over the image: no cross-linked
clusters, every file's chain as long as its size, no lost clusters, and
the clean bit set. **Deterministic deaths**, too: a test-only argument
(devmgr passes it only to test disks' fat, as other test powers) makes
fat exit at its Nth held write, at its commit, between commit and send,
and between send and reply, once each, while a script writes, renames,
unlinks and creates; each must leave the same files and checksums as a
run without deaths. And the bad-request rule: a test-only request that
crashes fat crashes it twice and is then answered `ERR_IO`, with fat
still serving.

**QEMU, the mixer** (tools/mixer-restart-test.sh): a test program plays
a 16-bit **ramp** (every sample one more than the last) at 0 dB, which
the mixer passes bit-exact, through QEMU's WAV capture as
`tools/mixer-test.sh` does; the mixer is killed 20 times while it plays.
The capture must be the ramp with no sample missing, repeated or zero,
except where QEMU's own `hda_audio_overrun` trace says QEMU dropped a
buffer (the existing tolerance). Today a kill breaks the ramp (the
frames the dead mixer had taken are never played, and the stream itself
fails), so the test fails before the work and passes after.

**On the PC** (Q7): `storm -c /usb0/big.bin /data/big.bin 0` and then at
1, 10 and 100 kills a second, both fats; `fsck_msdos -n` on the Mac
afterwards. The mixer: `music start`, `storm mixer 2 60`; you listen,
and the mixer's counters say whether any period ran out (`late` stays 0,
`min_lead` above the guard: `play -s` shows them for a `play`, and the
mixer logs them per restart).

## Risks

- **FatFs's internals.** Adoption depends on which structs hold FatFs's
  state. FatFs is vendored unmodified at R0.16; the layout utest fails on
  any upgrade that changes it, and the deterministic-death tests catch a
  missed piece. Fallback: Q3's (B).
- **The hold as a write-ahead buffer** changes the timing of every write
  (data copied once more, sent at the request's end). Expected to be in
  the noise next to the stick; F2 measures it before anything depends on
  it.
- **The mixer's gap budget** is real time: a successor scheduled late on
  a busy PC could miss 122 ms. The spare and the mixer's priority keep it
  far away; the restart line logs the lead left each time.
- **Duplicate replies** in a few-instruction window (above): harmless
  for every client today; a new hand-written client that doesn't check
  txids would see a stray message.
- **QEMU's audio drops** look like gaps; the existing overrun trace tells
  them apart.
