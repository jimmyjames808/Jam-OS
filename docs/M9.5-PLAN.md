## Window scaling and bulk rings, as built

After the join (2026-10-03, the owner's call): on the PC, `speed` against
`tools/speed.py` on the Mac over 5 GHz Wi-Fi gave 8.9 MB/s out and 9.0
in at a 6.4 ms round trip, which is 64240 bytes a round trip: the
unscaled window was the limit, not the link.

- **Window scaling** (RFC 7323, `port/lwipopts.h`): on, our shift 6, a
  window of at most 2 MiB when the peer scales too (both SYNs carry the
  option), at most 64240 (44 segments) when it doesn't. The window is
  still the rx ring's room, now counted in 64-byte units and rounded
  down, so it never exceeds the room. lwIP checks that a connection's
  window was left alone until its SYN's options were parsed, so a
  connection netstack opens sends its SYN with lwIP's unscaled 65535 and
  takes its own window when the SYN-ACK comes (`tcp_connected_cb`, before
  the handshake's ACK goes; no byte can come before that ACK); a
  listener's connections take theirs before the SYN-ACK, as before.
- **Send buffers**: a connection keeps at most its tx ring's size unacked
  in lwIP, at least 64240 bytes (`stack_tcp_send_buffer`: lwIP's
  `snd_buf`), and four times that in segments; so a program's default 16
  KiB ring keeps the 64240 in flight it had, and a bulk program's 2 MiB
  ring 2 MiB.
- **lwIP's memory shared out** (`stack_tcp_room`): the heap grew from 1
  to 6 MiB and the segment pool from 1024 to 4608. TCP's bytes never take
  the heap's last 64 KiB (UDP, ARP, ICMP, TCP's own ACKs and FINs), and a
  connection's bytes past its first 64240 (a bulk sender's) never the
  last 1 MiB beyond that or the last 1024 segments: every connection's
  first 64240 bytes, 15 at full speed at once, as before scaling. The
  bulk senders share the rest, about 4.9 MiB: two with 2 MiB in flight at
  once. A connection that finds its share taken waits (tcp.c's retry) for
  a turn that finds memory freed, an ack anywhere or a connection lwIP
  finishes alone.
- **Rings**: up to 2 MiB each (were 256 KiB); an opener's sockets 8 MiB
  (were 2), all sockets 24 MiB (were 16), ordinary programs 16 of them
  (were 8): the network's services keep their 8 MiB. The default stays
  small (16 KiB tx, 64 KiB rx). `<net.h>`'s `NET_TCP_BULK` (2 MiB) is
  what the bulk programs ask for: `fetch` its rx ring, `speed` both (and
  `speed -l`'s listener), `serve` its clients' tx rings. `update` is UDP
  and keeps its 64 KiB rx ring. A listener's connection whose tx ring
  doesn't fit its opener's bytes or the shares gets the default tx ring
  instead of a reset (only tx: the SYN-ACK announced the rx ring's
  window), so `serve`'s first three clients get 2 MiB rings and the rest
  16 KiB ones.

**Memory, worst case** (all of it static or counted; lwIP's own structs
measured: a pcb 248 bytes, a segment 32, a pbuf 24):

| | before | after |
|---|---|---|
| one connection's rings | 4 KiB + 2 x 256 KiB = 516 KiB | 4 KiB + 2 x 2 MiB = 4.0 MiB |
| one connection's bytes in lwIP | 64240 (+ ~6% for segments) | its tx ring, 64240 to 2 MiB (+ ~6%) |
| an opener's rings | 2 MiB | 8 MiB |
| all rings | 16 MiB (8 programs', 8 the services') | 24 MiB (16 programs', 8 the services') |
| lwIP's heap | 1 MiB | 6 MiB |
| segments, pcbs, receive buffers | 32 + 93 + ~200 KiB | 144 + 93 + ~200 KiB |
| netstack's network memory in all | about 17.3 MiB | about 30.5 MiB |

The ring pages are charged to netstack's job only as they are touched, and
shrunk to nothing when a socket closes; the heap's pages stay once
touched.

**Tests**: utest `nettcp_window_scale` (a connection netstack opens to a
peer that scales: the option on both SYNs, the window the 2 MiB ring's
room in 64-byte units, 146000 bytes in flight from the peer all in the
ring; more than 64 KiB in flight from netstack with a 256 KiB tx ring, at
most 64 KiB with a 64 KiB one; an unscaled peer gets 64240),
`nettcp_listen_scale` (the SYN-ACK carries the option only if the SYN did,
its window field unscaled; the scaled window after it),
`nettcp_heap_shares` (three bulk senders whose peer never acks take 4.6
MiB and leave the last part; an ordinary connection still sends) and
`nettcp_bulk_rings` (five connections to a listener with 2 MiB tx rings:
three get them, two the default, none reset). `tools/tcppeer.py` and
`tools/tcprelay.py` scale too, and `tools/tcp-test.sh` adds 8 MiB each way
with 2 MiB rings to a server whose ACKs come 20 ms late: the peer must see
the guest's window over 64 KiB and more than 64 KiB in flight both ways
(QEMU, 2 CPUs, 2026-10-03: a 2 MiB window and 2 MiB in flight from the
guest; on the default rings 64 KiB at most).

**On the PC**: `speed` both ways against `tools/speed.py` (and `net
stats`'s TCP lines). The window is no longer the limit (2 MiB in 6.4 ms
is over 300 MB/s); the Wi-Fi link is, so expect several times the 9 MB/s,

[below](#out-of-order-segments-and-sack-as-built)).

## Out-of-order segments and SACK, as built

The PC's first `speed` against `tools/speed.py` on the Mac over 5 GHz
Wi-Fi (the owner, 2026-10-04; a 6 ms round trip, UDP showing the link
tops out about 47 MB/s with heavy loss at full blast): PC to Mac 34.7
MB/s (8.9 before window scaling), Mac to PC only 11.6 (9.0). The PC's
receive path dropped nothing (the card: nothing missed, no ring full, no
descriptor short; netstack: nothing refused), but lwIP dropped every
segment that came past a hole after a Wi-Fi loss (`TCP_QUEUE_OOSEQ` 0) and
sent no SACK, so with a 2 MiB window the Mac resent everything from the
hole on. The Mac's side keeps them and sends SACK, which is why the other
direction was fine.

- **Kept** (`port/lwipopts.h`: `TCP_QUEUE_OOSEQ` 1): a segment past a hole
  waits on its connection's out-of-order queue, in its frame's receive
  buffer, until the hole is filled; then lwIP hands the whole run to the
  rx hook in order. lwIP takes no byte past the window, which is the rx
  ring's room, so the ring always takes the run whole.
- **Said** (`LWIP_TCP_SACK_OUT` 1, `LWIP_TCP_MAX_SACK_NUM` 4): netstack's
  SYN offers SACK-permitted, and a listener's SYN-ACK when the SYN did;
  then each segment past a hole is answered at once with an ACK carrying
  SACK blocks (RFC 2018) for the kept ranges, newest first, four at most
  (all an ACK's 40 bytes of options hold). lwIP sends SACK on bare ACKs
  only, which is all a receiver needs. lwIP ignores the SACK blocks it
  receives: what netstack sends is resent the old way.
- **Bounded** (`stack.c`, `stack_ooseq_pbufs`, through lwIP's
  `TCP_OOSEQ_PBUFS_LIMIT`, asked after each segment it queues; past it
  lwIP drops the queue's top, and tells the peer by dropping those SACK
  blocks too): a connection's queue holds at most its window in full
  segments of receive buffers (a peer sending tiny segments fills buffers,
  not bytes; 45 for a 64 KiB ring, 1437 for 2 MiB), and its share of the
  pool, shared out as the heap is for sending: the queues never hold the
  pool's last 128 buffers (`STACK_RX_KEEP`: every frame needs one while
  lwIP looks at it, and the pool was 128 before), so at most 1024 together
  (`STACK_OOSEQ_MAX`); a connection's buffers past its first 45
  (`STACK_OOSEQ_FIRST`, a default ring's window) never the last 256
  beyond those (`STACK_OOSEQ_KEEP_BULK`). So one bulk receiver keeps up to
  768 (1.1 MB, about 25 ms of a 47 MB/s link: four round trips of the
  PC's Wi-Fi), and five more connections still keep their first 45 each.
  `TCP_OOSEQ_BYTES_LIMIT` is the window, which lwIP already holds it to.
  A connection netstack let go of (`stack_tcp_release`) gives its queue
  back at once and keeps none after (nobody would read it). lwIP's own
  backstops stay: a queue nothing joined for six retransmission timeouts
  is dropped, and `PBUF_POOL_FREE_OOSEQ` frees one if a frame ever finds
  no buffer (`stack_poll` runs the check every turn; the shares keep it
  from happening).
- **Segments**: each queued segment also takes one of lwIP's segments,
  so the pool grew by 1024 (to 5632), and `stack_tcp_room`'s sending
  shares count only the 4608 they had: the queues' segments (no more than
  the receive buffers in use) are left out of what senders see as used.
- **First, still**: netstack's checks before lwIP (a segment with no ACK
  flag, RFC 9293; an ACK of bytes never sent or long acked, RFC 5961) run
  on every frame before lwIP sees it, so such a segment is dropped and
  counted, never kept.
- **Counted**: `net stats` has a TCP part now, with "held past a hole now"
  (the receive buffers on queues) and "queue at its limit" (times a queue
  was cut), from `<net.h>`'s `tcp_ooseq_held` and `tcp_ooseq_cut` (two of
  the counters' reserved words).

**Memory, worst case** (one receive buffer is 1560 bytes: a 24-byte pbuf
and 1536; a segment 32 bytes; a pcb 40 more for the queue and the SACK
ranges):

| | before | after |
|---|---|---|
| held past a hole, one connection | nothing (dropped) | its window in full segments, at most its share: 45 buffers (69 KiB) for a 64 KiB ring; 768 (1.1 MiB) for a 2 MiB one |
| held past a hole, all connections | nothing | 1024 buffers (1.5 MiB), the pool's last 128 always free |
| receive buffers | 128, 195 KiB | 1152, 1.7 MiB (all touched at start: lwIP links them) |
| segments, pcbs | 144 + 93 KiB | 176 + 108 KiB |
| netstack's network memory in all | about 30.5 MiB | about 32.1 MiB |

A hostile peer with a hole that never fills pins at most its
connection's share; with many connections (to a listener, or a program's
own to a hostile server) the 1024, after which every connection's
segments past a hole are dropped as before; what comes in order is never
refused for it.

**Tests**: utest `nettcp_ooseq_sack` (kept, the SACK blocks exact, the
ring exact once filled, the checks before lwIP first, a peer without
SACK, the listener's offer) and `nettcp_ooseq_bounds` (tiny segments held

**On the PC**: `speed 10.2.21.174 -r` (the Mac sends) should come near

## Ports below 1024, as built

The owner's call (2026-10-02): `serve file 80` should work, without
giving every listening program the ports where the well-known services
live. A narrower permission, by the listen permission's own mechanism:

- **`/svc/net-low`**: a fourth shared channel init makes once and
  keeps (netstack's SR_USER + 4, init's `net.c`), published like
  `/svc/net-listen`. An opener that came through it is a listening one
  that may also take ports 1 to 1023 (`struct opener`'s `low`, set at
  connect from the channel's `struct opener_kind`), for UDP and TCP,
  never the DHCP ports 67 and 68 (netctl's socket's). Everyone else is
  refused below 1024 as before (`listen_may_bind`, `listen_may_accept`,
  which now takes the port). `listen.c` serves both listening channels.
- **The want**: `svc net listen low` grants `/svc/net` and
  `/svc/net-low`, shown to the owner as "net, accepting
  connections from the network, below port 1024 too"; the bare `svc
  net-low` is no want (`wants.c`, `tools/checkwants.py`).
- **Who may have it**: `tools/checkwants.py` allows the line only under
  `user/services/`; bin/serve's list has it (init's grants for it too),
  and nothing else in the boot image. **`allow` refuses it for every
  program on `/data`**, approval or not, as `/svc/serve`, `net-sys` and
  `dns-sys`: a program that anything with the stick could have written
  could pose as one of the system's services to the network (a web
  server on 80, a time server on 123, the ssh M13 brings on 22) or take
  such a port before the service that owns it starts, and a question at
  the prompt wouldn't make that plain to the owner. A program that needs

## The contract: a socket's rings

Its home is the header comment of
[`user/include/sockring.h`](../user/include/sockring.h); this is the
summary and the reasons.

**A socket** is its channel (control calls in IDL; closing it closes
the socket), **one VMO** and **two events**. The VMO is a header page,
then the tx ring (the program writes, netstack reads), then the rx ring
(netstack writes, the program reads). Each ring is a power of two from 4
KiB to 2 MiB (256 KiB until window scaling), chosen at the open.

**The header page** (`struct sockring_page`, six cache lines):

| Line | Written by | Fields |
|---|---|---|
| `info` | netstack, once | magic `SRNG`, framing, tx and rx sizes |
| `tx_prod` | the program | `count` (bytes since the open, never wraps), `waits`, `flags` (`SOCKRING_END`) |
| `tx_cons` | netstack | `count`, `waits` |
| `rx_prod` | netstack | `count`, `waits`, `flags` (`SOCKRING_END`) |
| `rx_cons` | the program | `count`, `waits` |
| `status` | netstack | `state`, `error`, `changes`; `rx_dropped`, `tx_refused`, `ring_errors` |

Byte b is at `data[b % size]`; empty when the counts are equal, full
when they are a ring apart. Each side writes only its own lines and
never reads them back.

**Framing**, one per socket:
- **datagrams** (UDP, now): records of a 16-byte header (`addr`, `port`,
  `len`, `flags` 0, `reserved` 0) and `len` bytes (0 to 1472), padded to 16.
  A record's header never straddles the ring's end; its bytes may. tx:
  where to (0:0 = the connected peer); rx: who from.
- **bytes** (TCP, track C): plain bytes in order, any size read or
  written. The end of a direction is `SOCKRING_END` on the producer's line
  (tx: the program shut down sending, netstack sends FIN after the last
  byte; rx: the peer's FIN). No push flag: netstack sends what it finds
  when it looks, so publishing is the push. Track C fills in only
  netstack's TCP side and `<net.h>`'s calls: the ring code is built and
  tested.

**Events**: `to_stack` (the program signals: `SIG_TX` bytes or the end,
`SIG_RX_ROOM`) and `to_prog` (netstack signals: `SIG_RX`, `SIG_TX_ROOM`,
`SIG_STATE`). A side signals only when the other's `waits` says it
sleeps (publish, fence, read the flag; raise the flag, fence, look
again), so no system call per datagram while data flows. An event can't
say its signaller died: a program waits on `to_prog` and its channel's
`SIG_PEER_CLOSED` together, on a port (libos does; track D builds on it).

**Rights the program gets**: the VMO read, write, map (no resize, no
duplicate: netstack's mapping is safe); `to_stack` signal only;
`to_prog` wait and signal; each with transfer, as an IDL reply moves
handles.

**Backpressure, and netstack never waits**: netstack takes from a tx
ring only what it can send now and leaves the rest (the program sees a
full ring, sleeps for `SIG_TX_ROOM`); a datagram that doesn't fit an rx
ring is dropped and counted (`rx_dropped`); a stream's bytes that don't
fit stay in lwIP (the window shrinks) until `SIG_RX_ROOM`. At most a
ring's worth from one socket a turn.

**Trust**: netstack keeps its own counts and copies; the program's count
is read once a look and clamped (backwards: nothing; over a ring ahead:
a ring; off a record's edge: rounded down; each counted); a record's
header is read once (two atomic words) and checked; a bad one loses the
framing, so everything published so far is dropped and counted once;
bytes are copied out before use (lwIP gets copies); bytes after the end
are ignored; `to_stack` is bound PERSISTENT, so signals coalesce. The
program's side runs the same code.

**Memory and limits**: the VMO is netstack's (pages charged to its job
as they are touched). Each socket's bytes (header page + both rings)
count against its opener, at most 8 MiB, and all sockets, at most 24
MiB (2 and 16 MiB until window scaling): over either,
`ERR_NO_RESOURCES`. UDP's default rings are 16 KiB tx
and 32 KiB rx (52 KiB a socket; 11 full datagrams to send, 22 to
receive, hundreds of small ones), so M9's 32 sockets fit in 1.7 MiB, about
what their 32-datagram queues could hold before. The DHCP socket counts
against the total only.

**Close and errors**: closing the channel ends the socket; netstack
unmaps the VMO and **shrinks it to 0 bytes** before closing its handles,
so no page charged to netstack outlives the socket in a program's hands
(otherwise a program could keep rings after their sockets and grow
netstack's charge without bound). A program unmaps first (libos does).
What netstack had not taken from tx is dropped; a stream that must know
its bytes went sets the end and waits for the status line. `state`
(`OPEN`; streams also `CONNECTING`, `CLOSED`) and `error` change with
`SIG_STATE`; a refused tx record (bad address, no route) counts in
`tx_refused` with its reason in `error`, so `net_sendto` returns once the
datagram is in the ring, not once it left.

**Why this shape** (decisions):
- *One VMO per socket*, not one per ring (netdev has two): one map and
  one handle, and the two rings have the same trust.
- *Byte counts*, not netdev's fixed 2 KiB slots: a DNS reply takes 80
  bytes, not 2 KiB, and the same count logic serves the byte stream, where
  reads and writes of any size are the point.
- *netstack makes and maps the VMO*: the side that maps must hold the
  only resize right (the mixer, which can't, reads client rings with
  `vmo_read` instead).
- *The control stays IDL*: open, connect, state, and later TCP's calls are
  rare; only data moves to the rings.

## Track A part B, built: UDP on the rings, and the fair shares

**The calls** (`abi/idl/net.idl`): on an opener's channel `udp_rings(port,
tx_bytes, rx_bytes)` (ordinal 15) opens a socket with its rings in one
call (the socket's channel, the ring VMO, `to_stack`, `to_prog`, the port
and the sizes; 0 for UDP's defaults), so a service's loop opens one
without waiting (dns: `net_udp_open_async`, `net_udp_opened`). On a
socket's channel `sock_rings(tx_bytes, rx_bytes)` (20) gives a socket made
another way its rings: netctl's DHCP socket (`net_sock_adopt`), and `udp`
sockets. `sock_send_to` and `sock_recv` (16, 17) are gone: one data path.
`sock_state`'s `queued` is the bytes waiting in the rx ring.

**netstack** (`sock.c`): a received datagram goes straight into the
socket's rx ring (no heap copy, no queue), published once a turn
(`progs_flush`, after the card's frames went to lwIP) and the program
signalled only if its flag is up; one that doesn't fit is dropped and
counted. The tx rings are read in turn from where the last turn stopped,
32 records a socket a turn, and only while the card's tx ring has room
for a datagram (and an ARP request); with none, the rest waits in the
rings and netstack raises its flag on the card's ring (`dev_tx_wait`)
until the driver's `NETDEV_SIG_TX_ROOM` (`dev_tx_room_came`). A record is
checked on its copy; a refused one counts in the socket's status line,
which is written before the count that shows it taken, so a blocking
sender that sees its datagram taken sees why it was refused. A broken
ring (a count out of range, a bad record) is not looked at again until the
program signals. A socket whose opener goes is ended (lwIP's socket and
its port freed, its status CLOSED, `ERR_PEER_CLOSED`) but its rings and
channel stay, still counted, until the program closes its end: then the
VMO is unmapped and shrunk to 0 bytes. lwIP's UDP sockets went from 33 to
49 (`lwipopts.h`: the only change in track C's file).

**libos** (`<net.h>`: `user/lib/net.c` the opener's calls and opening,
`user/lib/netsock.c` a socket's datagrams): every function kept, both
forms. `struct net_sock` holds the mapping and the events. The blocking
`net_sendto` waits for room, puts the datagram, then waits until netstack
took it, and returns netstack's reason if it refused it (as the call did
before); `net_recvfrom` and `net_sock_wait` sleep on a port of the
socket's own (`to_prog` and the channel's `SIG_PEER_CLOSED`). A loop binds
a socket on its own port with `net_sock_bind` (dns, dhcp, netlog), or puts
it in a wait set (`net_sock_waitable`). The `waits` flags are set only by
what sleeps (the blocking calls raise and lower theirs; `net_sock_bind`
raises rx's for good; a wait set its own); `net_sendto_async` and
`net_sock_take` never touch them. `net_recv_arm` is gone (nothing to arm).
`net_udp_open_rings` takes ring sizes: `bin/update` asks for a 64 KiB rx
ring, its window of 32 replies.

**The fair shares** (the M9 review's question B). netstack can tell
channels apart, not programs, so a class is fixed at connect by the
shared channel an opener came through, as track B's listen permission is
(one mechanism: `struct opener_kind`, progs_connect's context):
`/svc/net` and `/svc/net-listen` make ordinary openers, `/svc/net-sys`
(SR_USER + 3, published by init) system ones. init grants `/svc/net-sys`
to dns, netlog and sntp, `bin/update` asks for it (`svc net-sys`:
`tools/checkwants.py` allows it only under `user/services/`, and `allow`
refuses it for `/data`), and libos's `net_svc`/`net_svc_open` prefer it.
Ordinary openers together get 24 of the 32 openers, 24 of the 48 sockets,
48 of the 64 requests in flight and 8 of the 16 MiB of ring bytes; the
per-opener limits stay (16 sockets, 8 requests, 2 MiB). A refusal for the
share is `ERR_NO_RESOURCES`, counted (`refused_shares`). One program can
still take its class's whole share by opening many openers (netstack
can't tell its openers from another program's); the network's services
are what the reserve protects. Track C applies the same to TCP
connections; dns's own openers (`/svc/dns`) got theirs in the join
([below](#the-join-as-built), item 4).

## Track B, built: SNTP and the listen permission

**SNTP** (`user/services/sntp`, [ARCHITECTURE](../ARCHITECTURE.md#networking)
has the details). init starts `bin/sntp` once `/data`'s settings are read,
unless `ntp = off`, with `ntp.server` as its argument, `/svc/net` and
`/svc/dns`, and the root with `RIGHT_ROOT_CLOCK` only (the one new holder
of that power). Choices:
- *The server, by default the gateway, then `pool.ntp.org`.* The gateway
  (10.2.21.1 on the owner's VLAN 21, the DHCP lease's router) needs no
  DNS and keeps the traffic on the VLAN; if it gives no time (not every
  router serves NTP: not checked on the owner's yet), the pool through
  `/svc/dns`. `ntp.server` (an address or a name) replaces both.
- *Started by init after `/data`, waiting for the address itself*
  (`net_wait_up`), as netlog does: init can't know when DHCP has an
  address without asking netstack inside its loop.
- *Never set from a reply it can't match*: each request's transmit
  timestamp is 64 random bits (our own time is never sent), and only a
  reply whose origin is exactly those bits is looked at further (mode,
  version, stratum, leap, both timestamps, root distance under 1 s, the
  order of the times, a round trip under 5 s, 2026 to 2199). A
  kiss-o'-death counts only with a matching origin. Everything else is
  counted and said in one line per request.
- *Steps.* The first set takes the whole step (the RTC may be hours out:
  local or UTC), but over 60 s only when a second reply to a second nonce
  agrees within 1 s. Then hourly, at most 5 s a time (a cut-short step
  asks again in 64 s). No slewing: the kernel's clock is an offset, and a
  few ms an hour don't need it. Retries: 4 requests 2 s apart a server,
  rounds backing off from 16 s to 1024 s.
- *The kernel*: `wallclock_set` takes one flag, `WALLCLOCK_NET`, kept
  until a set without it. init uses it to keep the network's time when
  `/data` comes back (it used to read the RTC again, which would put the
  RTC's error back until the next sync); `date -z` keeps it; `date -r`
  says "set from the network (sntp)".

**The listen permission.** The roadmap's shape, `svc net listen` in a
program's list, granted through `allow`:
- *How netstack learns it*: init makes a second shared channel next to
  `/svc/net`'s, publishes it as `/svc/net-listen` and gives netstack its
  server end (SR_USER + 2). An opener that connects through it is marked
  at connect (`struct opener`'s `listen`); nothing a program sends can set
  it, and a program has the channel only if the shell gave it: `svc net
  listen` grants `/svc/net` and `/svc/net-listen` (the bare `svc
  net-listen` is refused by `wants.c` and `tools/checkwants.py`, so the
  owner always reads the long form). `allow` shows "net, accepting
  connections from the network".
- *UDP today*: below 1024 never (but netctl's DHCP socket); 1024 to 49151,
  the ports servers are known by, only with the permission; port 0 or
  49152 and up for anyone. A program without it still receives replies on
  its own sockets: UDP can't tell a reply from an unasked datagram, so
  what the permission guards is a port someone else could know in
  advance. bin/dns's random source ports moved from 1024-65535 to
  49152-65535 (16384 ports and a 16-bit id: about Linux's own range).
- *TCP (track C)*: `listen_may_accept(o)` in `user/services/netstack/listen.h`
  is the hook `listen` and `accept` ask; nothing else to add.
- *Files outside B's own*: two small hooks in track A's `clients.c`
  (`progs_connect`/`progs_shared_dispatch` exported, the bind check

## Track D as built: waiting on many sockets

`<netwait.h>` (libos, `user/lib/netwait.c`): a **wait set**, one thread
waiting on up to 256 sockets and other handles at once. Its header comment
is the contract; this is the summary and the reasons.

**The calls.** `netwait_create(max)`, `netwait_destroy`;
`netwait_add_sock(set, {rings, to_prog, ch, tx_need}, interest, user,
&id)` and `netwait_add_handle(set, {h, read, write, hup signals}, ...)`;
`netwait_modify(id, interest)`, `netwait_remove(id)`, `netwait_touch(id)`;
`netwait_wait(set, deadline, out[], max, &n)`, which returns the ready
entries (`id`, `ready` bits, `error`, `user`); `netwait_wake(set)` from any
thread. Interest is `NETWAIT_READ` and `NETWAIT_WRITE`; reports add
`NETWAIT_HUP` and `NETWAIT_ERROR` (always) and `NETWAIT_RX_END`.

**Readiness is computed, not remembered.** A socket is readable when its
rx ring has a datagram or a byte or its end, writable when it is OPEN, its
tx direction not ended and its tx ring has room for `tx_need` (a largest
datagram, or a byte), hung up when the stream is CLOSED or netstack's end
of the channel closed, in error when it closed with one (or netstack is
gone: `ERR_PEER_CLOSED`). Each comes from the rings' counts, the end flags
and the status line at the moment the set looks; a signal only says which
entry to look at. So the set is **level-triggered** (an entry is reported
by every wait while it is ready), and a signal that was coalesced, early
or doubled can't lose readiness or invent it.

**One port, O(ready).** Each socket entry binds its `to_prog` event
PERSISTENT and its channel ONCE for `SIG_PEER_CLOSED`, with keys that
carry the slot and a generation (a removed entry's late packets are
ignored). An entry is pending (looked at by the next wait) or armed
(asleep). A pending entry found not ready is armed: the event's bits
cleared, the `waits` flags of its interest raised with `sockring_sleep`
(raise, fence, look again), and one more look; a reported entry stays
pending. A wait therefore looks at the entries that got a packet and the
ones still ready, never at all of them (`netwait_api` shows one look per
wait with 64 entries asleep). It costs no system call per datagram while
data flows: flags are lowered whenever an entry is reported, so netstack
signals only sockets the set is asleep on.

**Rules** (the header has them): a set belongs to one thread; a socket is
in one set; while it is, its waits go through the set (calls that don't
wait are fine; after a call that slept on the socket by itself,
`netwait_touch`); remove a socket before closing it (a binding holds its
channel object, so netstack wouldn't see the close).

**Costs.** Two bindings a socket (about 2.4 KiB of the job's message
bytes), one a handle; a look at a socket is a few loads, arming one is one
system call, a look at a handle is one.

**What track A exports for it** (built in A's part B): `net_sock_waitable`
fills a `struct netwait_sock` from a `<net.h>` socket, and the forms
that don't wait never touch the `waits` flags; a blocking call on a
socket in a set lowers the set's flag (`netwait_touch` after it).

**Not built yet:** a listening TCP socket (track C: if `accept` is a
deferred IDL call, its channel is a handle entry for `SIG_READABLE`);
edge-triggered entries (`EPOLLET`) and one-shot ones; tasks
(`<jam/task.h>`) waiting on a set.

### How M13's `poll` and `select` map onto it

- A thread's descriptors live in one set of the C library's, kept between
  calls: an entry is added the first time a descriptor is polled and
  removed when it is closed; a call changes only the interests that
  differ (`netwait_modify`). Descriptors not in this call's list get
  interest 0 and their HUP and ERROR reports are dropped.
- `poll(fds, n, timeout)`: the deadline is `now() + timeout` ms
  (`DEADLINE_NEVER` for -1, a past deadline for 0), `out` has room for n.
  `POLLIN` is `NETWAIT_READ`, `POLLOUT` `NETWAIT_WRITE`, `POLLHUP`
  `NETWAIT_HUP`, `POLLERR` `NETWAIT_ERROR`, `POLLRDHUP` `NETWAIT_RX_END`;
  a descriptor with no entry (a closed one) is `POLLNVAL`. Regular files
  are always ready and need no entry.
- `select`: read sets are `NETWAIT_READ`, write sets `NETWAIT_WRITE`; an
  ERROR marks the descriptor readable and writable (select's rule: a
  failed connect shows as writable); except sets are always empty (no
  urgent data). The error itself is `SO_ERROR`, from the report's `error`
  or the status line.
- Pipes and terminals are channels: handle entries (`SIG_READABLE`,
  `SIG_WRITABLE`, `SIG_PEER_CLOSED`).
- `EINTR`: a signal handler's wake-up is `netwait_wake`
  (`ERR_CANCELED`).
- `epoll` maps one to one: `epoll_ctl` add, mod and del are add, modify
  and remove, `epoll_wait` is `netwait_wait`, level-triggered as here.

## Track C part 1, built: TCP inside netstack

lwIP's TCP is on, and netstack can run connections on byte-stream
sockrings, without the program-facing calls yet (part 2: net.idl's TCP
methods, `<net.h>`'s stream calls, limits per opener with the fair
shares, the QEMU test). Three layers, each with one job:
- **`port/lwipopts.h`**: TCP on (lwIP 2.2.1's `tcp.c`, `tcp_in.c`,
  `tcp_out.c` added to third_party, unmodified). MSS 1460 (one frame,
  never fragmented); a window of 44 segments (64240 bytes, the largest in
  whole segments without window scaling, which stays off) and the same
  for `TCP_SND_BUF`; a window announced as soon as it grew by a segment
  (RFC 1122's rule; lwIP's default would stall a 4 KiB ring);
  out-of-order segments dropped, not queued (nothing a peer sends can pin
  receive buffers; the peer's fast retransmit covers a LAN's rare loss);
  no SACK, timestamps or keepalive; TIME_WAIT 60 s (`TCP_MSL` 30 s, as
  Linux); the first retransmission after 1 s (RFC 6298); initial sequence
  numbers from the kernel's random source (`LWIP_HOOK_TCP_ISN`: lwIP's
  own is a guessable counter). Memory, all static: 384 pcbs (netstack's
  256 connections and 128 for half-open and closing ones), 16 listeners,
  backlogs up to 16, 1024 queued segments, and the heap grown from 64 KiB
  to 1 MiB for TCP's unacked copies (15 connections sending at full speed
  at once; past that bytes wait in their rings).
- **`stack.c`'s TCP edge** (`stack.h`, no lwIP type outside): connect,
  listen, the accepted connection taken, room and send (copies, all or
  nothing), push, window given back (`stack_tcp_recved`), shutdown (FIN),
  release, abort, and hooks for bytes in, the peer's FIN, connected, acked,
  gone (with why) and accepted. Two choices in it: **the window is the rx
  ring's free room** (each connection's window set to min(64240, its rx
  ring) before its SYN or SYN-ACK; a listener's through lwIP's per-pcb
  slot, read before the SYN-ACK goes), and **pcb priorities** (listeners
  and their half-open connections, and connections netstack let go of,
  lowest; programs' connections normal): lwIP recycles a low one when its
  pool is full, so a SYN flood can never take a program's pcb and the pool
  never limits programs' connections: netstack's own counts do, which is
  where part 2's fair shares and the system services' reserve go. And a
  check lwIP lacks (RFC 5961 section 5): a segment with bytes or a FIN
  whose ACK is for bytes never sent, or older than any window the peer was
  given, is dropped before lwIP, which would see the bad ACK but still
  take the bytes (a blind injection then needs only a sequence number in
  the window).
- **`tcp.c`** (netstack's connections, `tcp.h` has the model, `ntcp_*`):
  a table of 256 connections and 16 listeners, joined to their rings.
  Bytes in go straight into the rx ring; the window is given back only as
  the program takes them, so a slow reader stops its sender at once and no
  byte waits in lwIP (what the contract calls "stays in lwIP" is a peer
  past the window: lwIP drops it, or keeps one segment as refused data).
  Past half the window (less a segment) netstack keeps the rx producer's
  `waits` up, so the program's reads wake it and the window opens again
  at once; below that the peer knows of half a window at least and keeps
  sending. Bytes out: what the tx ring held when netstack looked, at most
  what the peer's window takes and a segment more (so a peer that stops
  reading holds no more than its own window in lwIP); an empty ring
  raises the tx consumer's `waits`. END is FIN both ways. CLOSED when both
  FINs went and ours was acked (`error` OK), at once on a reset
  (`ERR_PEER_CLOSED`; `ERR_NOT_FOUND` for our SYN), a timeout
  (`ERR_TIMED_OUT`), the address going (`ERR_BAD_STATE`), or a program's
  ring count out of range (`ERR_OUT_OF_RANGE`: the stream can't be trusted,
  it is reset before a byte of it is sent). Accepted connections get their
  rings from the listener's owner when lwIP finishes the handshake (a
  hook), so bytes that come before the program accepts wait in the ring.
  A connection freed with bytes unread is reset, else it closes with a FIN
  and lwIP finishes the closing alone. Hooks run inside lwIP and only move
  bytes and note work; `ntcp_work` does the rest from the loop, every
  connection noted at most once a turn.
- **Known, from lwIP**: a segment with no ACK flag still had its bytes
  taken (RFC 9293 says drop it); dropped before lwIP since the join
  ([below](#the-join-as-built), item 5).

## Track C part 2, built: TCP for programs

On A's rings and B's listen permission: `net.idl`'s TCP methods, `<net.h>`'s
stream calls, the limits with the fair shares, and the QEMU test.
- **The calls** (`abi/idl/net.idl`, ordinals 21-23; named so the generated
  `net_*` calls stay clear of `<net.h>`'s): on an opener's channel
  `tcp(address, port, tx, rx)` (a connection, answered at once,
  CONNECTING: the socket's channel, its stream rings' VMO and events, its
  port) and `tcp_listener(port, backlog, tx, rx)` (only an opener of
  `/svc/net-listen`: `listen_may_accept`; ports from 1024, 0 for one from
  49152); on a listener's channel `accept(timeout_ms)`, a `later` method
  answered when a connection comes, one at a time a listener. Shutdown is
  no call: SOCKRING_END on the tx ring (`net_shutdown`) is the FIN, as the
  contract says. A TCP socket's channel answers `sock_state`; closing it
  closes the connection (a reset if bytes it never read are left).
- **A listener in a wait set**: libos always sends `accept` without waiting
  (`net_tcp_accept_send`), so its answer lands on the listener's channel;
  the channel is a handle entry (`net_listener_waitable`: READ is
  SIG_READABLE, HUP SIG_PEER_CLOSED) and `net_tcp_accept_take` reads the
  answer. No new netwait code.
- **netstack** (`tcpsock.c`, 560 lines): sockets and listeners in tables
  of their own, port keys with bit 63 set (progs.h's low byte was full);
  a listener's connection gets its socket, channel and rings in tcp.c's
  rings hook when lwIP finishes the handshake, charged to the listener's
  opener; `accept` hands over the channel's other end and the rings'
  handles. The ring memory code moved out of `sock.c` into `sockmem.c`
  (shared, no change for UDP). An opener's end closes its listeners and
  resets its connections, which stay (rings and all) until the program
  closes them, as UDP's do.
- **Limits and fair shares** (`<net.h>`): 64 connections an opener, 256 in
  all, 192 for ordinary openers together; 4 listeners an opener, 16 in
  all, 12 for ordinary openers; a backlog of 16 a listener, 128 together
  (lwIP's 128 pcbs beyond the connections), 96 for ordinary openers; ring
  bytes as UDP's (2 MiB an opener, 16 MiB in all, 8 MiB ordinary). A
  listener's connection that doesn't fit is reset and counted. Only
  `/svc/net-listen`'s openers listen today, and they are ordinary, so the
  listeners' and backlogs' reserve is for a system listener not built
  yet. Default rings: 16 KiB tx, 64 KiB rx (a whole window).
- **libos** (`user/lib/nettcp.c`; the socket's attach and waits shared with
  `netsock.c` through `user/lib/netint.h`): `net_tcp_open`,
  `net_tcp_wait_open`, `net_tcp_connect`, `net_tcp_wait_closed`,
  `net_tcp_status`; `net_write`/`net_write_some`, `net_read`/`net_read_some`,
  `net_shutdown`; `net_tcp_listen`, `net_tcp_accept` (blocking) and
  `net_tcp_accept_send`/`_take` (a loop or a wait set),
  `net_listener_waitable`, `net_listener_close`. Every wait clears
  `to_prog`'s bits before it looks, so a change after the look is a new
  signal.
- **Found on the way**: netstack must clear `to_stack`'s bits when it takes
  a program's signal (the binding fires on a rise only; a stream's second
  kick was lost); and the status line now stores `state` after `error`
  with release (`sockring.c`), so a program never reads CLOSED with the
  error from before.
- **Tests**: utest's `nettcp_limits`; `tools/tcp-test.sh` (bin/tcptest
  against `tools/tcppeer.py` through `tools/netpeer.py`): 10 MiB each way
  on one connection, and 20 connections at once to a guest listener served
  from one wait set, 256 KiB each way each, every byte checked on both
  sides, all frames VLAN 21 (and again with `QEMU_NET_VLAN=none`, all
  untagged). QEMU, 2 CPUs, 2026-10-02: 10 MiB out at 43-49 MB/s, in at
  31-35 MB/s ([TESTING](TESTING.md#area-scripts)).
- **Not done here**: TCP didn't hold back when the card's tx ring was
  full (a frame the ring refused went again only on TCP's timer): done in
  the join ([below](#the-join-as-built), item 5). The PC's numbers
  (BENCH.md) are the PC run's.

## Track E as built: fetch, serve and speed

Three programs on C's TCP and D's wait sets, and the Mac's side of them.
README's "The network" has the commands as the owner types them.

**`<http.h>`** (`user/lib/http.c`, `httpchunk.c`): the HTTP/1.1 both
programs share, pure functions over bytes. Strict and bounded, because
the other end may be hostile: a head is at most 16 KiB (`HTTP_HEAD_MAX`)
and 64 header lines, lines end in CRLF or LF (a bare CR, a control byte,
a folded line, a space before the colon: refused), numbers are plain
digits under 2^50, two Content-Lengths must agree, a Transfer-Encoding
other than `chunked` alone is refused, chunked wins over a length; a
chunk's size line is at most 256 bytes and the trailers 16 KiB. A request
is answered 400, 501 (not GET or HEAD) or 505 rather than guessed at; one
byte range is taken (`a-b`, `a-`, `-n`; anything else serves the whole
file, as RFC 9110 allows). `http://` only: https needs TLS, which is not
in M9.5 (`ERR_NOT_SUPPORTED`, said in plain words by both programs).

**`fetch <url> [file | -]`**: the shell's command checks the URL and
opens where the body goes, `<file>.part` (renamed into place once the
body is whole, removed otherwise, so a failed or stopped fetch leaves
nothing and an old file stays), or a channel for the pipe; bin/fetch
(`user/apps/fetch`, a helper as bin/play is) holds only that, its list
(`svc net`, `svc dns`: no listen permission) and the shell's stop channel,
so a hostile server reaches nothing else. One wait set holds the stop
channel and, in turn, the resolver's channel and the connection, so Ctrl+C
ends any wait at once (exit 130); each wait has a deadline: a name and a
connection 10 s each, the whole head 20 s, a body silent for 30 s. Up to
3 redirects (301, 302, 303, 307, 308; to `http://` only). The body by
Content-Length, chunks or the connection's end (the request says
`Connection: close`); bytes past a length are ignored. A progress line
every 2 s, the size, time and MB/s (10^6 bytes a second) at the end.
Exit 0, 1 (said why), 2 (usage), 130. For the pipe the shell got
`sh_run_helper_out`: the helper's lines go to the screen and the bytes on
a channel of its own into the pipe.

**`serve <file> [port]`**, `serve`, `serve stop [port]`: bin/serve
(`user/services/serve`) is a service init starts after netstack and keeps
(like the music player: the `serve` channel made once, `abi/idl/serve.idl`,
protocol 31, published as `/svc/serve`), so it outlives the shell and its
Ctrl+C. Its namespace is `/svc/net` and `/svc/net-listen` only, what its
list says (`svc net listen`): no mount. The shell opens the file read-only
and hands it over: `share(port, name)` answers with a channel on which the
shell writes the file's two handles (IDL arguments can't carry handles),
then reads the answer there once the server listens. The server serves
exactly that file, to every path asked: no directory, and nothing from a
request is ever opened. One thread and one wait set hold everything: the
control channels, each share's listener and file channel, every client;
reading the file is file.idl's read sent without waiting, one read at a
time a share, into its 64 KiB transfer buffer and from there into the
waiting client's chunk (the clients that want their next chunk queue).
Asking netstack for a listener waits for its answer, so a second thread
that serves nobody does it and hands the listener back (`listen.c`). Each
client: its head within 10 s, no progress for 30 s, the next request on
a kept connection within 15 s, else closed; keep-alive, but no pipelining
(a request sent before the last answer: that answer, then the close). At
most 4 shares (one listener each, on its own port), 24 clients, 16 a
share (more are reset). One log line per request (`serve: 10.2.21.174:
41000 GET /big.bin 200, 52428800 bytes`), at most 20 in 10 s, the rest
counted. A restarted server serves nothing (shares are not kept).
`/svc/serve` is the shell's alone: a program's list may not ask for it
(`tools/checkwants.py` allows it only in user/tests/, `allow` refuses it
for `/data`), since with it a program could put any file it can read on
the network without the listen permission.

**`speed`** (`user/apps/speed`, a helper of the shell's since the join:
Ctrl+C stops it with 130) and
**`tools/speed.py`** (plain Python, server and client): one TCP
connection, a 16-byte hello (mode, seconds), the sender's bytes for that
long and its FIN, the receiver's 16-byte report (bytes, ms) and its FIN;
each side prints its own count. UDP (`-u`): datagrams of 1472 bytes
from the PC to the Mac, then an end the Mac answers with what arrived.
`speed -l` listens (its list has `svc net listen`) for the Mac's
`speed.py client`, a test at a time until Ctrl+C. UDP from the Mac to the
PC is not built.

**Tests** (QEMU, 2 CPUs, 2026-10-02): utest `http_url`,
`http_response`, `http_chunks`, `http_request`, `http_fuzz`;
`tools/fetch-test.sh`, `tools/serve-test.sh`, `tools/speed-test.sh`
([TESTING](TESTING.md#area-scripts)) against real programs on the Mac
through the peer's new relay (`tools/tcprelay.py`: `--tcp-relay`,
`--tcp-forward`, `--udp-relay`, with a selftest), every frame VLAN 21 (the
peer's and the pcap's checks). QEMU's numbers through that relay: `speed`
TCP out 58 MB/s, in 70, UDP 109 with none lost; `speed.py client` 69 to
the guest, 47 from it; two `curl`s of a 5 MiB file from `serve` at once
28 MB/s in all; `fetch` of 6 MiB into `/data` 0.6-1.3 MB/s, bound by
writing the emulated stick, not by the network (the same TCP path
receives at 70 MB/s; `/data`'s writes are expected to speed up with the
`update -w` fix's larger fat writes). The PC's numbers go
in [BENCH.md](BENCH.md) from the PC run.

**Not built**: https (TLS); a listing of folders (never: one file); UDP
from the Mac to the PC in `speed`; `fetch` resuming a `.part`; a POST.
